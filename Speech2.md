We build capabilities once, as products, and deliver them everywhere as one experience. Once means the end of duplication. As products means our internal capabilities get the same care, ownership, and roadmaps as anything we ship to a user. Everywhere means any application, any workflow, and any AI agent can compose them. One experience means our users see a unified product, not our commit history.

Technically, this is a MACH architecture: microservices, API-first, cloud-native, and headless. But the acronym is not the vision. The vision is what the architecture makes possible, and it comes in three layers.


1. Capability platforms, treated as products. Today, capabilities exist in multiple incompatible versions across our portfolio, each maintained separately, each aging differently. Under MACH1, each core capability becomes a platform: one implementation, one product owner, one roadmap, published contracts, and real service commitments. The teams that consume a platform are its customers, and the platform team's job is to earn that business. This layer is where agility, resiliency, and deduplication live. When a capability exists once and is built well, it can be improved once, scaled once, hardened once, and trusted everywhere.

2. An AI orchestration layer. "AI ready" is not a feature we will bolt on at the end. It is a consequence of the first layer. AI agents can only act reliably through capabilities that are clean, contract-defined, observable, and governed. A tangle of duplicated, undocumented services is not just a maintenance burden; it is terrain no agent can safely operate in. The orchestration layer sits on top of our capability platforms and gives AI a governed way to perceive, reason, and act within our systems, with observability and guardrails built in from the start. This is what separates organizations that add a chatbot from organizations that are genuinely AI ready. We are building for the second category.

3. SuperApps that unify the experience. Our users should not need a map of our internal structure to do their work. A small number of SuperApps will compose platform capabilities into coherent, unified experience. When a user opens our software, they should see one product built by one organization, because that is what we are becoming.

What This Means for You

Teams will move toward one of two futures: platform product teams, building capabilities the entire organization depends on, or SuperApp experience teams, working closest to our users. Both are promotions in scope. Owning plumbing for one application is a smaller job than owning a capability for the whole enterprise. I want to address directly what everyone will quietly calculate when they hear the word "deduplication." Consolidating five implementations into one platform does not delete four teams' work. It changes what the work is.
