In the 1940s, pilots kept fighting in pursuit of a number: Mach 1, the speed of sound. The strange part is that their aircraft had plenty of power. Engines were not the problem. As these aircraft approached transonic speeds, the airframes themselves began to fail. Control surfaces reversed. Structures buffeted and shook apart. The machines had been designed for a slower regime of flight, and no amount of additional thrust could carry a subsonic airframe through the barrier.

Breaking Mach 1 did not require a bigger engine. It required a different airframe.

That is where we are as an organization. We were formed by bringing together several strong engineering organizations, each with its own applications, its own services, its own pipelines, and its own way of doing things. We have no shortage of engineering horsepower. What we have is an airframe assembled from several legacy fuselages, and as the demands on us accelerate, we can feel the buffeting: the same capability built five times in five places, users navigating our org chart instead of a product, and infrastructure that resists change instead of enabling it.

Project MACH1 is the redesign of the airframe. It is how we become an organization that can move at the speed our mission now demands.

Through December, we are doing foundation work, and I will be honest that much of it will not be glamorous. The entire portfolio moves onto Nitro and Platforma, with a hygiene standard attached: no end-of-life dependencies ride along. Apps arrive on the paved road containerized, contract-registered, and connected to unified identity. In parallel, a pathfinder team stands up our first anchor platforms and the next SuperApp shell, so that by December we can demonstrate, with running software, what this architecture is for.

Starting in January, the work becomes visible. From that point forward we hold ourselves to a rule: every piece of architectural work must surface an improvement a user can see.

One more aircraft belongs in this story. Fifteen years after the X-1 proved the barrier could be broken, a small pathfinder team designed the A-12, known by its project name: Oxcart. The name was a deliberate joke, the slowest vehicle imaginable attached to the fastest aircraft ever built. It did not merely survive past Mach 1 but lived there, cruising at more than three times the speed of sound at the edge of space. Its more famous descendant, the SR-71, carried the design into legend, but the Oxcart got there first, and it did its work in complete secrecy. The people who built and flew it changed what was possible without ever being able to say so.

Two details about the Oxcart matter to us. On the ground, it looked wrong. Its titanium panels fit loosely and it leaked fuel on the ramp, because every tolerance was engineered for the heat and speed of its operating regime, not for how it looked in the hangar. Our foundation quarter will be like that: unglamorous on the ground, designed for altitude. And over contested territory, it was never lost to hostile action. When fired upon, it did not turn to fight. It accelerated.

Breaking the sound barrier was not done by one pilot. It took engineers who rethought the airframe, crews who maintained it, and an organization willing to design for a regime of flight it had never operated in.
